How was this computed?

The ETA predicts the FAA’s nationwide lifting announcement, not an individual flight’s departure.

01

Quote midpoints

A YES contract pays $1 if the announcement happens before its deadline. We use the midpoint between the best YES bid and ask as a rough probability.

pi = (YES bidi + YES aski) / 2

For example: a 47¢ bid and 51¢ ask imply approximately 49%. Prices are market expectations, not calibrated odds.

02

Monotonic fit

“Before 3 a.m.” must be at least as likely as “before midnight.” If quotes disagree, a weighted isotonic regression finds the nearest nondecreasing curve. Narrower spreads receive more weight. Outcomes already settled at the observation time are fixed at 0% for NO and 100% for YES; the fit cannot move them.

min Σ wi(Fi − pi)²   with   Fi ≤ Fi+1
wi = 1 / max(aski − bidi, 0.02)²
03

Median

The forecast ETA is the median: half the implied probability lies before it and half after. We interpolate linearly between neighboring quoted or settled deadlines that bracket the percentile. There is no synthetic 0% point at the observation time.

t50 = ta + (0.50 − Fa) / (Fb − Fa) × (tb − ta)

A live worked example will appear here once prices load.

04

Probability range

The default middle 50% runs from the 25th to the 75th percentile. Middle 80% uses the 10th and 90th; middle 90% uses the 5th and 95th. If the available points do not bracket a percentile, we show “before” the first supported deadline or “after” the last. These are bounds, not exact times.

The PDF is the slope of the linearly interpolated CDF: (Fb − Fa) / hours between deadlines. Its height is probability per hour; its area gives interval probability. For example, a 10-point increase over half an hour is 20% per hour. Density can exceed 100% per hour over a short interval. We do not normalize the displayed area to 100% or assign a shape to unobserved tails. Negative raw slopes are shown for comparison but cannot be valid probability densities.

The bid/ask band and median sensitivity use separate monotonic fits of bids and asks. They show quote uncertainty, not a statistical confidence interval.

05

Mean and tail assumption

The average can be later than the median because of a small chance of a very long delay. When the first supported point A has probability 0%, we integrate from A. Otherwise the mean is unavailable because the lower tail is unknown. For the upper tail, you choose the average additional wait after the final deadline L.

E[T] ≈ A + ∫AL [1 − F(t)] dt + [1 − F(L)] × tail wait

06

Historical reconstruction

Every minute uses the same calculation: that minute’s closing YES bid and ask quotes, plus outcomes whose recorded settlement time is at or before the snapshot. Future outcomes never enter earlier estimates. Closed contracts remain in the table; their last quotes are labeled stale and excluded from the fit until a known settlement supplies an exact probability.

Estimates within the half-hour deadline window depend on linear interpolation, and observations after reopening measure market reaction rather than advance predictive accuracy.

What this assumes

Prices approximate probabilities, and linear interpolation fills the gaps between deadlines. API data can lag. Missing, crossed, stale or inactive quotes, and spreads of 98 percentage points or wider, are excluded. Passing a cutoff does not itself imply a NO settlement. Sparse deadlines limit timing precision.

Only an official nationwide lifting announcement qualifies. A future plan, regional relief, or your flight departing is a different event.
